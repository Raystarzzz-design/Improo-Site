What is the Ketogenic Diet?

01 — Introduction

What is keto?

The ketogenic diet — commonly called keto — is a way of eating that is very low in carbohydrates, moderate in protein, and high in fat. By drastically reducing carbohydrate intake, the body is pushed into a metabolic state called ketosis, where it burns fat as its primary fuel instead of glucose. It is not a new concept — the diet was originally developed in the 1920s as a clinical treatment for epilepsy.

02 — Mechanism

What happens in your body?

Under normal circumstances, your body runs on glucose — a sugar derived from carbohydrates. When you eat very few carbs (typically under 50g per day), your glucose supply runs low and your liver begins converting stored fat into molecules called ketone bodies. These ketones then serve as an alternative energy source for your brain and muscles.

This shift usually takes two to four days of consistent low-carb eating to occur. Once in ketosis, fat — both dietary and stored body fat — becomes the body's dominant fuel. Insulin levels drop significantly during this process, which also plays a role in how keto affects weight and blood sugar.

Ketosis is not a disease state — it is a natural metabolic response the human body has used for thousands of years during periods of low food availability.

03 — Suitability

Who is it for?

Keto is not a one-size-fits-all approach. It may be a better fit for some people than others — and that largely depends on your health situation, lifestyle, and personal relationship with food.

May be suitable for
  • People who have struggled with results from traditional calorie counting
  • Those with insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes (under medical supervision)
  • People who feel better with stable energy throughout the day
  • Those who find high-fat foods naturally satiating
  • People managing epilepsy (clinically validated use)
Less suited for
  • People with certain metabolic or kidney conditions — consult a doctor first
  • High-performance athletes who rely heavily on carbohydrates for fuel
  • Those who find eliminating carbs mentally or socially difficult to sustain
  • People with a history of disordered eating
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women without medical guidance
04 — Honest assessment

Pros & cons

↑ Potential benefits
  • Effective for short-to-medium term weight loss in many people
  • Can improve blood sugar stability and reduce insulin spikes
  • Many people report reduced hunger and fewer cravings
  • May improve HDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels
  • Clinically proven to reduce seizure frequency in epilepsy
↓ Potential drawbacks
  • The "keto flu" during the first 1–2 weeks (see below)
  • Difficult to maintain socially — most meals contain carbs
  • May raise LDL cholesterol in some individuals
  • Risk of nutrient deficiencies without careful planning
  • Long-term adherence rates are generally low
What is the keto flu? During the first one to two weeks of keto, many people experience symptoms including headaches, fatigue, irritability, brain fog, and nausea. This is commonly called the "keto flu" and occurs as your body adapts to using fat instead of glucose. It typically resolves on its own, and staying well-hydrated and maintaining electrolyte intake (sodium, potassium, magnesium) can help reduce symptoms.
05 — Research

What does the science say?

Research on ketogenic diets has grown significantly over the past two decades. The evidence is promising in some areas, more mixed in others.

[1]
Weight loss: short-term advantage, long-term parity

A 2013 meta-analysis published in the British Journal of Nutrition found that very low-carbohydrate ketogenic diets led to greater weight loss compared to low-fat diets at 12 months — even when calories were not explicitly restricted. However, the difference narrowed over longer periods, suggesting adherence is the main determinant of long-term success.

Bueno et al. (2013). British Journal of Nutrition, 110(7), 1178–1187. PubMed ID: 23651522
[2]
Blood sugar & type 2 diabetes: strong evidence

A 2020 review published in Nutrients concluded that ketogenic diets can meaningfully reduce HbA1c levels and fasting blood glucose in people with type 2 diabetes, sometimes allowing for medication reduction under supervision.

Batch et al. (2020). Nutrients, 12(7), 2005. PubMed ID: 32645999

On cholesterol, the picture is less clear. Keto reliably improves HDL ("good") cholesterol and lowers triglycerides. However, LDL ("bad") cholesterol may increase in some individuals. Anyone with existing cardiovascular risk factors should consult a physician before starting.

06 — Summary

The short version

  • Keto is a very low-carb, high-fat diet that shifts your body into a fat-burning state called ketosis.
  • It can be effective for weight loss and blood sugar management, particularly in the short to medium term.
  • The "keto flu" is a common and temporary adjustment phase during the first week or two.
  • It is not suitable for everyone — people with certain health conditions should seek medical advice before starting.
  • Like any diet, the biggest predictor of success is whether you can actually sustain it long-term.
