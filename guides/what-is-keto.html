01 — Introduction

What is keto?

The ketogenic diet — commonly called keto — is a way of eating that is very low in carbohydrates, moderate in protein, and high in fat. By drastically reducing carbohydrate intake, the body is pushed into a metabolic state called ketosis, where it burns fat as its primary fuel instead of glucose.

It is not a new concept. The diet was originally developed in the 1920s as a clinical treatment for epilepsy, and it remains one of the most researched dietary interventions in modern medicine.

Key insight

Keto is not a wellness industry fad. It has over a century of clinical use — originally developed by physicians at Mayo Clinic to treat drug-resistant epilepsy in children.

02 — Mechanism

What happens in your body?

Under normal circumstances, your body runs on glucose — a sugar derived from carbohydrates. When you eat very few carbs (typically under 50g per day), your glucose supply runs low and your liver begins converting stored fat into molecules called ketone bodies. These ketones then serve as an alternative energy source for your brain and muscles.

This shift usually takes two to four days of consistent low-carb eating. Once in ketosis, fat — both dietary and stored body fat — becomes the body's dominant fuel. Insulin levels drop significantly during this process, which also affects weight and blood sugar.

Ketosis is not a disease state. It is a natural metabolic response the human body has used for thousands of years during periods of low food availability — such as winter or extended fasting.

03 — Suitability

Who is it for?

Keto is not a one-size-fits-all approach. It may be a better fit for some people than others — depending on your health situation, lifestyle, and personal relationship with food.

May be a good fit
  • People who haven't seen results from traditional calorie counting
  • Those with insulin resistance or type 2 diabetes (under medical supervision)
  • People who feel better with stable, consistent energy levels
  • Those who find high-fat foods naturally satiating
  • People managing epilepsy (clinically validated use)
Less suited for
  • People with metabolic or kidney conditions — consult a doctor first
  • High-performance athletes who rely on carbs for explosive output
  • Those who find carb elimination socially or mentally difficult
  • People with a history of disordered eating
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding women without medical guidance
04 — Honest assessment

Pros & cons

↑ Potential benefits
  • Effective for short-to-medium term weight loss
  • Can improve blood sugar stability and reduce insulin spikes
  • Many people report reduced hunger and fewer cravings
  • May improve HDL cholesterol and triglycerides
  • Clinically proven to reduce seizure frequency in epilepsy
↓ Potential drawbacks
  • The "keto flu" during the first 1–2 weeks
  • Difficult to sustain socially — most meals contain carbs
  • May raise LDL cholesterol in some individuals
  • Risk of nutrient deficiencies without careful planning
  • Long-term adherence rates are generally low
What is the keto flu? During the first one to two weeks, many people experience headaches, fatigue, irritability, brain fog, and nausea as the body adapts. It typically resolves on its own. Staying well-hydrated and maintaining electrolyte intake — sodium, potassium, magnesium — significantly reduces symptoms.
05 — Research

What does the science say?

Research on ketogenic diets has grown considerably over the past two decades. The evidence is strong in some areas, more nuanced in others. Here is what the most cited studies actually found.

[1]
Weight loss: short-term advantage, long-term parity

A 2013 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Nutrition found that very low-carbohydrate ketogenic diets led to greater weight loss than low-fat diets at 12 months — even without explicit calorie restriction. The difference narrowed over time, suggesting adherence is the main predictor of long-term success.

Bueno et al. (2013). British Journal of Nutrition, 110(7), 1178–1187. PubMed ID: 23651522
[2]
Blood sugar & type 2 diabetes: strong evidence

A 2020 review in Nutrients concluded that ketogenic diets can meaningfully reduce HbA1c levels and fasting blood glucose in people with type 2 diabetes, sometimes allowing for medication reduction under supervision.

Batch et al. (2020). Nutrients, 12(7), 2005. PubMed ID: 32645999

On cholesterol, the picture is less clear. Keto reliably improves HDL and lowers triglycerides — but LDL may increase in some individuals. Anyone with cardiovascular risk factors should consult a physician before starting.

What this means for you

The research suggests keto works well as a short-to-medium term tool — particularly for weight loss and blood sugar control. Long-term, what matters most is whether you can realistically maintain it.

06 — Summary

The short version

  • Keto is a very low-carb, high-fat diet that shifts your body into a fat-burning state called ketosis.
  • It can be effective for weight loss and blood sugar management, particularly in the short to medium term.
  • The "keto flu" is a common and temporary adjustment phase — manageable with hydration and electrolytes.
  • It is not suitable for everyone — people with certain health conditions should seek medical advice first.
  • Like any dietary approach, long-term success comes down to whether you can actually sustain it.
